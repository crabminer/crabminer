Abstract—This page describes, and runs, a small economy of crab robots. Along the foot of the screen, five kinds of crab turn buried nodules into metal ingots. Drill crabs uncover nodules. Hauler crabs stack them. Crusher crabs press them into meltable bars. Smelter crabs refine the bars in a lava cavern. Energy bots carry the lava's heat back to the crew as charge. No crab is given orders. Each follows a short rule that reads only what is in front of it, and the assembly line emerges from those rules. The economy is also a game. A crew has twelve crabs to divide among the five roles, and a scored shift of two simulated minutes counts the ingots it delivers. The simulation is deterministic, so every reader who fields the same crew gets the same score. A search of all 6,188 possible crews found the best score to be 29 ingots.

Index Terms—Assembly line, bottleneck, division of labor, serious games, stigmergy, swarm robotics.

I. Introduction

The sea floor at the foot of this page is a working model. It extends the crab stage of the Micro-Miners proposal, in which small robots prove themselves on the seabed before any attempt underground or in space. Here the crabs do more than gather. They run a complete line from a buried nodule to a finished ingot, and they do it without a foreman.

The page has two uses. Read as a paper, it sets out the roles, the rules each role follows, and the arithmetic that limits the line. Used as a game, it asks one question: with twelve crabs, which crew makes the most ingots in one shift? Fig. 1 holds the controls. Outside a scored shift, a click or tap anywhere drops an extra nodule for the haulers.

A. Drill Crabs

The drill crab is yellow and carries a bit under its shell. It picks a spot in the nodule field, walks there, and drives the bit into the sand. The crab shakes, sand sprays, and the water it churns turns in a spiral over the hole. The nodule appears as the spiral dies away. Three holes in ten strike a rich pocket and give a second nodule. Drill crabs stop when 24 nodules already lie on the sand, because more would only be litter.

B. Hauler Crabs

The hauler is the orange crab with a hopper on its back. It walks to the nearest nodule that no other hauler has claimed, lifts it, and looks for the next. With three aboard it walks to the ore pile and stacks them one at a time into a pyramid five nodules wide. The pile holds 15. A hauler that arrives at a full pile waits beside it, and the waiting is itself a signal, as Section III explains.

C. Crusher Crabs

The crusher is steel blue with oversized claws. It stands beside the ore pile and never walks the field. When the pile holds two nodules, it takes both and hammers them between its claws until they fuse into one grey bar. It sets the bar on the bar stack, which holds 10. Crushing is the slowest single task on the line at 4.5 seconds. It also halves the count of items, so one crusher keeps pace with two or three haulers.

D. Smelter Crabs and the Lava Cavern

At the right end of the floor a basalt outcrop opens into a cavern, and the cavern holds a pool of lava. The pool swells, bubbles, and throws embers, and its light falls on whatever stands at the rim. The smelter crab is charcoal, with glowing claws and a visor. It takes a bar from the stack, walks to the rim, and holds the bar in the lava for three seconds. The bar brightens and comes out as an ingot. The smelter carries the ingot back to the collector's chute, and the collector sends it up the riser to the surface. Each ingot that leaves is one point.

E. Energy Bots

Every working crab carries a battery, shown as a small gauge on its shell. A full battery lasts 40 seconds of activity. Below 20% the gauge flashes red and the crab drops to about a third of its speed. The energy bot is the small green machine with four charge cells on its back. It finds the crab with the lowest battery under 60%, runs to it, and passes one charge across in a bright arc. After four charges it returns to the lava to refill from the heat.

Energy bots make nothing, yet a crew without them fails. In the search described in Section V, the best crew scored 29 with its two energy bots, 21 with one, and 13 with none.

III. Swarm Rules

No crab knows the plan, and nothing in the program schedules the line. Each crab runs the one rule in Table I, and each rule reads only local facts: the nearest nodule, the height of a pile, the level of a battery. The piles do the coordinating. A full ore pile stops the haulers. An empty one stops the crushers. A full bar stack stops the crushers again from the other side. This is stigmergy, the name Grassé gave to coordination through traces left in the shared workplace [1], and it is a founding idea of swarm intelligence [2].

The roles here are fixed, which is the simplest form of division of labor. Insect colonies do something subtler: each worker has a threshold for each task and takes it up when the need rises past that threshold [3]. Robots have been built to allocate foraging work in the same ant-like way [4]. In this model the reader plays the part of the threshold. When a pile overflows, the reader moves a crab to the stage behind it.

Local rules suit the sea floor for a practical reason. A crew that needs no central controller needs no constant link to one, and it keeps working when a member is lost. Remove a crab in Fig. 1 while the line is running and the others carry on.

IV. The Economy

The currency is the ingot. Two nodules make one bar and one bar makes one ingot, so an ingot costs two nodules. The line can deliver no faster than its slowest stage, a point made for factories in The Goal [5]. The stock in front of each stage shows which stage that is, and Table II lists the signs.

Distance is a cost as well. The track is 1,000 units long. The nodule field runs from 20 to 600, the ore pile stands at 660, and the lava begins at 880. Working crabs walk 60 units a second and energy bots 95. A hauler loaded in the middle of the field needs about five seconds to reach the pile, so hauling takes more crabs than its short task times suggest.

Power is the overhead. Every energy bot is a crab that carries nothing, and every crab left uncharged works at a third of its pace. The best crew is the one that balances the four stages and then spends just enough on power.

V. The Game

Crew. A crew has at most twelve crabs. Any split among the five roles is allowed, including none in a role. The plus and minus buttons in Fig. 1 change the crew while the line runs. A new crab sinks from the surface and lands in a puff of sand.

Shift. A scored shift lasts two simulated minutes. It starts from the same state every time: eight nodules on the sand, empty piles, and full batteries. The crew is locked until the shift ends, and clicks do not drop nodules. The score is the number of ingots delivered.

Fairness. The economy runs on its own fixed track, in fixed steps of a sixtieth of a second, with its own seeded random numbers. The screen only draws it. The same crew therefore earns the same score on a phone and on a desktop, and at every viewing speed. The speed buttons change how fast the shift is shown, and a shift at four times speed takes thirty seconds.

Medals. Bronze is 20 ingots, silver is 25, and gold is 28. The starting crew of ten scores 22. A search of every crew of up to twelve crabs, 6,188 in all, found the highest possible score to be 29, and exactly one crew reaches it.

Competition. After a shift the panel shows a result line such as D2 H3 C2 S2 E1 = 22 ingots. The letters are the five roles in order. Because the score depends only on the crew, two players can compare result lines directly, and anyone can check a claim by fielding the same crew.

VI. What Is Real and What Is Not

The nodules are real. Polymetallic nodules lie on abyssal plains and consist mainly of manganese and iron oxides, with nickel, copper, and cobalt in smaller amounts [6]. The lava is real too: basaltic lava erupts on the sea floor at roughly 1,100 to 1,200 °C.

The smelting is a game rule. Copper melts at 1,085 °C, which basaltic lava can reach. Manganese melts at 1,246 °C and nickel at 1,455 °C, which it cannot. Turning an oxide ore into metal also takes chemistry, a reducing agent, and heat alone will not do it. The deposits in the model never run out, the crabs never break, and sunlight reaches a floor that in life is dark. The model is honest about one thing: how a line of simple workers behaves when its stages are out of balance.

VII. Conclusion

Five rules and two piles are enough to produce an assembly line that looks managed and is not. The same arrangement makes a fair game, because a deterministic line has one best crew and many near misses. The reader who finds the crew that scores 29 has learned what the model has to teach: find the stage that sets the pace, feed it, and pay for power last.
