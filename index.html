It's a Crab Mining Game.

You run a crew of robot crabs on the sea floor. They find metal, dig it up, smelt it in a lava cave, and send it up a pipe to the surface. Every ingot that goes up is money. Crab profit!

Here is how you play

  1. Hire crabs in the Crew tab. Each kind does one job: scouts flag metal, drills dig it up, haulers carry it, crushers press it into bars, and smelters melt the bars into ingots.
  2. Keep them running. Energy bots charge the crew from a Stirling engine beside the lava. Repair bots fix broken drill bits and worn energy bots, and every repair uses up one ingot from your stockpile.
  3. Sell metal upward. The riser sends your stockpile to the surface and pays the market price. Choose how many ingots to keep back for repairs.
  4. Grow. Spend profit on more crabs, a bigger den, the middle and back dunes, and better gear. The advisor tells you which stage is slowest.

Here is why it's fun

  • It's a tiny city builder on the sea floor. You watch a whole production line come together from nothing.
  • No crab takes orders. Each one follows a simple rule, and you balance the crew. Fix the slowest stage and money starts flowing.
  • Mining is a gamble. Some holes come up dry, bits snap, and metal prices rise and fall, so no two runs are the same.
  • The crabs are very expressive. They stick their tongues out while drilling, sweat at the lava, grumble at a dry hole, and dance when you rank up.
  • Climb six ranks from Sand Scraper to Crab Tycoon, then try to get there faster.

Crabminer: A Crab Mining Game, Its Rules, and a Review of Its Economy

crabminer

Build Systems for Embedded Hardware, United States

Abstract—Crabminer is a city builder played on the sea floor. The player runs a crew of robot crabs that turns buried metal into money. Scout crabs scan the dunes and flag likely deposits. Drill crabs dig them up, and a hole can come up dry. Haulers, crushers, and smelters carry the ore through a pile, a press, and a lava cave until it is refined metal on a stockpile. A riser sends that metal to the surface at the market price. Drill bits break, and a broken bit draws three times the power. Energy bots wear out, and they recharge themselves at a Stirling engine beside the lava. Repair bots fix both kinds of breakdown, and each repair uses up an ingot that could otherwise have been sold. The player hires crabs, holds back metal for repairs, and builds upgrades, climbing six ranks from Sand Scraper to Crab Tycoon. This paper sets out the rules and reviews the economy using a deterministic simulation. The review measures every role, searches all 8,008 possible ten-crab crews for the best one, and plays the game with a simple automatic player to check the pace of progress.

Index Terms—Bottleneck, city builder, division of labor, game economy, serious games, stigmergy, swarm robotics.

I. The Game

The game opens on three layers of dunes, cut from paper and stacked one behind another. The front dune holds the processing line: an ore pile, a crushing yard, a bar stack, a stockpile of refined metal, and the collector at the foot of the riser. A basalt outcrop on the right holds a pool of lava with a Stirling engine beside it. The middle and back dunes start out roped off. They hold richer deposits, and the player can buy the claims to them.

The player starts with nine crabs, room in the den for ten, and 120 credits. The aim is to earn money by selling metal upward. Lifetime earnings set the player's rank, and each new rank pays a bonus of a tenth of its threshold. The ranks are Pebble Boss at 150 credits, Nodule Baron at 400, Ingot Magnate at 1,000, Lava Lord at 2,500, and Crab Tycoon at 6,000. Reaching Crab Tycoon wins the game, and the time taken is the player's score. Play can continue afterwards.

The player has three kinds of lever. The Crew tab hires and retires crabs. Each further crab of a role costs 15% of the base price more than the one before, and a retired crab refunds half of what it cost. The Build tab buys upgrades. The stockpile control in the top bar sets how many ingots to hold back from sale for repairs. The Ledger tab holds the analysis: what each stage could produce, what it is producing, and where the money goes. An advisor at the top of the Crew tab names the slowest stage in plain words.

III. Breakdowns and Repairs

Drill bits. Every hole carries a 4.5% chance of breaking the bit. A drill crab with a broken bit keeps working, but badly. Its holes take 60% longer, it strikes 40% less often, and while it drills it draws three times its normal power. Its face shows the strain. Hardened bits cut the chance to 2.5%, then to 1.2%.

Energy bots. Every charge an energy bot hands over carries a 3% chance of wearing it out. A worn bot moves at under half speed and takes twice as long over each charge. It still works, so a crew without repairs fades slowly rather than stopping dead.

Repairs cost metal. A repair bot takes one ingot from the stockpile for each fix, so every repair is a sale that does not happen. This is why the stockpile has a reserve. Ingots above the reserve go up the riser. Ingots at or below it wait, ready for the next repair. If the reserve is zero, the riser can empty the stockpile and leave a repair bot standing at it, waiting for metal while the bits stay broken.

Power. Every crab except an energy bot carries a battery that lasts about 45 seconds of work. Below 20% it slows to about a third of its pace and its eyes droop. The Stirling engine turns the lava's heat into charge at 32 charges a minute, banks up to 12, and charges two bots at a time. If more charge is needed than the engine makes, energy bots queue at the engine however many there are. Upgrades raise its output to 56 and then 90 a minute.

IV. The Economy

Income. The riser sends one ingot to the surface every 3 seconds while the stockpile holds more than the reserve. That caps sales at 20 ingots a minute until the riser pump is upgraded to 1.8 and then 1.0 seconds. Each ingot fetches the current metal price. The price centres on 12 credits. A slow swell moves it about 18% over two and a half minutes, a faster ripple adds 7%, and a seeded random walk drifts it up to 15% either way, all within 6.6 to 18 credits.

Spending. Credits buy crabs and upgrades (Table II). Repairs are paid in metal, not credits. There is no upkeep, so a crew never loses money. A badly balanced crew just earns slowly.

Distance. The track is 1,000 units long. The field runs from 20 to 580, and the line takes up the stretch from 600 to the lava at 888. Crossing from the back crest to the front of the dunes costs as much as 300 units of track. Crabs walk 60 units a second, scouts and repair bots 78, and energy bots 95. Deep deposits are richer and larger, but every nodule from them takes a longer walk.

V. Economy Review

The review was run in Node.js on the same simulation script the page runs. The simulation is deterministic: it steps in fixed sixtieths of a second with its own seeded random numbers, so a given crew and seed always give the same result. Each figure below is ingots sold per minute, measured after a two-minute warm-up.

What one crab can do. Table III gives each role's output in ingots a minute when every other stage is oversupplied. Crushers are the most productive crabs and haulers the least, so a balanced line needs about two and a half haulers per crusher. A scout's flags keep a little over one drill busy on the front dune, and nearly two once the back dune is claimed. Single-seed figures like these vary by about a tenth from run to run. Without scouts, drills strike so rarely that each one makes only about 0.6 ingots a minute. One energy bot keeps about five or six crabs charged, and the base Stirling engine supports about twelve.

A working configuration. All 8,008 ways to split ten crabs among the seven roles were simulated for eight minutes. The 25 best were then rerun with four more seeds and averaged. The best crew is one scout, two drills, two haulers, one crusher, two smelters, one energy bot, and one repair bot, at 7.1 ingots a minute (about 85 credits a minute). That is the starting crew plus a second smelter. In the starting crew the single smelter sets the pace, so the advisor's first tip is to hire another one. The best ten-crab crews with no scout or no energy bot make only 4.2 and 4.0 ingots a minute. Skipping repairs looks almost free over eight minutes: the best crew without a repair bot makes 6.8. Over twenty minutes, though, every drill in it ends up with a broken bit, and it falls to 4.5 against 7.1 for the best crew. Repairs are an investment that pays off over time.

Bigger crews. Hill-climbing from a balanced start, one crab moved at a time, found these crews. With 14 crabs: 2 scouts, 2 drills, 3 haulers, 1 crusher, 3 smelters, 2 energy bots, and 1 repair bot, at 11.3 a minute. With 18 crabs and the first riser upgrade: 2, 4, 4, 2, 3, 2, and 1, at 18.0. With 30 crabs and every upgrade: 2, 5, 8, 4, 6, 4, and 1, at 38 a minute. That is about 450 credits a minute at the average price.

Pace. An automatic player that always fixes the advisor's bottleneck, and spends spare money on upgrades, reached Pebble Boss at 1.8 simulated minutes, Nodule Baron at 3.5, Ingot Magnate at 6.7, Lava Lord at 14.8, and Crab Tycoon at 25.5. At 4× speed that is just over six minutes of real time. Income grew from about 75 credits a minute in the first minute to about 350 with a full den of 30, which it filled by minute 17. The early ranks come every two minutes or so and the last one takes about eleven, so the game gets going quickly and still leaves a goal to chase.

Changes made in review. The first draft had three faults, and the numbers above come from the corrected rules. Bits broke on 7% of holes, so repairs ate a third of all metal. They now break on 4.5%, which costs about a sixth. Scouts found so much that one scout could feed six drills, so scans now take longer and succeed less often. The Stirling engine upgrade only shortened a refill, which changed almost nothing because energy bots spend most of their time walking. The engine now makes a fixed number of charges a minute, so power has a real limit that the player can see and raise.

VI. Swarm Rules

No crab knows the plan. Each one runs the rule in Table I, and each rule reads only local facts: the nearest nodule, the best flag, the height of a pile, the level of a battery. The piles and the flags do the coordinating. This is stigmergy, the name Grassé gave to coordination through traces left in a shared workplace [1], and it is a founding idea of swarm intelligence [2]. Insect colonies divide work by giving each worker a threshold for each task [3], and robots have been built to share foraging in the same way [4]. Here the player acts as the threshold, moving crabs to whichever stage is short. The line can deliver no faster than its slowest stage [5], and the Ledger tab exists to show which stage that is.

VII. What Is Real and What Is Not

The nodules are real. Polymetallic nodules lie on abyssal plains and consist mainly of manganese and iron oxides, with nickel, copper, and cobalt in smaller amounts [6]. Lava erupts on the sea floor too, and basaltic lava is roughly 1,100 to 1,200 °C. A Stirling engine runs on a temperature difference rather than on fuel, so one placed between lava and cold seawater is at least sound in principle.

The smelting is a game rule. Copper melts at 1,085 °C, which basaltic lava can reach. Manganese melts at 1,246 °C and nickel at 1,455 °C, which it cannot. Turning an oxide ore into metal also needs chemistry, a reducing agent, and heat alone will not do it. Deposits do not run out across the dunes as a whole, and sunlight reaches a floor that in life is dark. The model is faithful about the economics: breakdowns tax output, repairs consume product, and the slowest stage sets the pace.

VIII. Conclusion

Seven local rules, three piles, and one stockpile are enough for a production line that looks managed and is not. Uncertain mining, breakdowns paid for in metal, and a fluctuating price turn it into a game with real decisions. The review finds that the economy works: every role pays for itself, every upgrade raises output, and a sensible player grows from nine crabs to thirty in under twenty minutes of simulated time. The lesson is the same as before: find the stage that sets the pace and feed it. Pay for power and repairs next, because skipping them costs more than they do.
